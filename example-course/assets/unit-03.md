## concept: dialing-in
- quiz: You change grind finer AND raise the dose, and the shot improves. What do you actually know? | a: nothing attributable — two variables moved, so you cannot say which caused it, or whether one helped while the other hurt. The whole method is one change per shot, tasted and named before the next | distractor: M1
- quiz: Your shot runs in 18s and tastes sour and thin. Name the single change you make, and what you expect to happen to time and taste. | a: grind finer — more resistance, so the shot slows toward ~25-30s, extraction rises, and the sourness should give way to sweetness. One variable, one prediction, then taste | distractor: M2
- quiz: A recipe from a roaster says 18g in, 36g out, 28 seconds. You hit all three numbers exactly and the cup is bitter. What's the error in the question? | a: the numbers were never the goal — they are one bean's coordinates on someone else's grinder. Bitter means over-extracted: coarsen or shorten regardless of what the card says. The cup is the instrument; the recipe is a starting guess | distractor: M2
- example: worked | A dial-in is a loop with a written record, not a fiddle. Start: 18g in, 36g out, 32s, tastes bitter and dry. Hypothesis: over-extracted. Change ONE thing — coarsen two steps. Result: 26s, tastes balanced, slight sourness at the finish. Attribute: coarser grind lowered extraction, slightly too far. Change one thing: finer by one step. Result: 29s, sweet, no sour edge. Three shots, three single changes, each one tasted and named. Compare to changing grind and ratio together on shot two — the cup improves and you have learned nothing you can repeat tomorrow.
- apply: Deliberately mis-set your grinder three steps too coarse, then dial back to a shot you'd serve. Log each shot: the one variable you changed, the time, the taste, and your attribution — before you pull the next one.

## rubric: dialing-in
- claim: one variable changes per shot, and the result is tasted and attributed before the next change
- claim: taste is the instrument and the target — time and ratio are diagnostics that explain the taste, never the goal themselves
- claim: a prediction is made before the shot (what should happen to time and taste), so a surprise is informative rather than just another data point
- avoid: M1
- avoid: M2

## interleaved
- problem: A shot pulls in 19s and tastes bitter — the combination that "shouldn't" happen, since fast usually means under-extracted. What single cause explains both, and what do you change? | concepts: dialing-in, channeling
